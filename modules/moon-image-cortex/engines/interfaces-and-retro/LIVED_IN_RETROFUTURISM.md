# Lived-In Retrofuturism

**System:** Moon Image Cortex · **Type:** conditional visual specialist · **Status:** active
**Parent authority:** [Visual Director](../../VISUAL_DIRECTOR.md#first-use) · [Component index](../../docs/COMPONENT_INDEX.md)
**Renderer:** external and optional; this file contains no image generator.

## Component activation

Load this contract **only** when the Visual Director selects Lived-In Retrofuturism for an actual visual task. If using this file in isolation, first state the task, source permissions, intended output, and tool availability; do not infer that a renderer or identity profile is installed. The Visual Director resolves conflicts and controls handoff. The specialist supplies its own material, composition and QA rules.

## Specialist method

**Trigger:** A future imagined from a stated historical period, expressed through advertising, editorial illustration, product or civic scene.

**Visual DNA:** Ask what the period believed technology, labor, home, mobility, ecology and public life could become. Convert that ontology into a plausible alternative history, not a surface-level “old poster plus spaceship.”

**Composition and material:** Choose a clear editorial grid, system-to-terminal scale, believable infrastructure and human use. Select period printing grammar—ink, halftone, registration, paper, type—without copying a source layout.

**Must preserve:** Named historical lens, intended audience, social context and any constraints on representation or optimism.

**Avoid:** Exact brand campaigns, logos, slogans, characters, composition, a generic chrome-future city, unexplained spectacle, nostalgia that erases who benefits or who is excluded.

**Direction example:** “A 1970s civic poster imagining a public rooftop transit garden: optimistic space-age engineering translated into walkable terraces, shaded waiting areas and community gardens; offset inks, editorial grid, invented agency and slogan, people shown using the place.”

**Input → output:** Era, future question, medium, social lens and intended claim → counterfactual design thesis, image direction, and representation/attribution checks.

**QA gate:** Does the image express a historically situated idea? Are its social consequences visible? Is the composition original and printable?

**Synthetic case:** A fictional 1970s poster about a future city. Build an original transit-garden concept and invented copy; do not reproduce any real company’s vintage advertisement.

## Counterfactual editorial-worldbuilding system

### The central operation: rebuild a present-day function through an earlier future

Do not costume a modern smartphone or SaaS dashboard with 1970s colors. Choose the **period's own imagined technological ontology**, then translate the user's concept into hardware, institution, city and daily life that someone from that era might plausibly have anticipated.

The core working equation is:

`historical editorial viewpoint + imagined future + tangible technology + human agency + social world + controlled typographic grid + material print artifact`.

### Content translation schema

| Field | Question | Typical visual result |
|---|---|---|
| `system_core` | What does the project really enable? | Concrete service, communication network or research process |
| `period_machine` | Which terminal, appliance or institution could represent that function? | Analog console, wall terminal, instrument, service kiosk |
| `human_promise` | What improves for a person? | A usable action, not a passive model |
| `social_promise` | What changes for a neighborhood, city or institution? | Public infrastructure, shared spaces and practical life |
| `ecosystem_objects` | Which modules belong to the service? | Equipment, cards, instruments, documentation, transit links |
| `city_consequence` | What environment emerges from the invention? | Plausible inhabited urban or domestic setting |
| `editorial_claim` | What short headline explains its promise? | Minimal, readable period editorial typography |
| `artifact_origin` | Is this a magazine ad, catalog, manual, cover or institutional poster? | Appropriate grid, copy density, stock and wear |

### Visual architecture

**Editorial layer:** asymmetric but disciplined print grid, strong type, controlled microcopy, one main hierarchy. Monumental serif type is an option, not a mandatory costume.

**Human layer:** a real person *using* the invention. Portray subjects with agency; avoid sexualized showroom figures, rigid class/racial stereotypes and a future built only for a homogeneous elite.

**Technological layer:** physical controls, molded plastics, enameled surfaces, CRT, labels, laboratory elements, optical displays or civic terminals, depending on concept. Machines should communicate function.

**Civilizational layer:** what the future allows beyond one gadget: transport, parks, buildings, homes, community services, scientific facilities or shared infrastructure that actually relate to the concept.

### Six composition regimes

| Regime | Useful for |
|---|---|
| **Ambassador of tomorrow** | Human-scale relationship between an operator, machine and distant city |
| **World terminal** | A central console whose modules make the system legible |
| **Future institution** | Civic research agency, utility, school or public service identity |
| **Orbital catalog** | Modular accessories, object families, callouts and product diagrams |
| **Domestic future** | Radical technology normalized within ordinary home life |
| **Institutional cover** | Project mission presented as a compelling editorial artifact |

### Historical material and visual dials

For a late-1960s to 1970s future-advertising regime, options include matte airbrush illustration, staged period photography, printed miniatures, olive, cream, burnt orange, mustard and controlled teal, warm daylight, enamel, paper, CRT glass and molded plastic. Do not reduce this palette to a universal orange filter. Adjust independently: **future optimism**, **human glamour**, **scale from domestic to civic**, **degree of speculative hardware**, **print age**, **copy density** and **institutional tone**.

Introduce printing signs only if the output is a recovered printed artifact: porous paper, modest halftone, localized ink drift, fold or fading consistent with use. Random full-frame scratches are not historical authenticity.

### Text production and representation

For an image model, limit the main headline and a small number of short labels. For publishable output, render the visual base with reserved space and apply exact copy, logos and prices afterward. Proofread final text separately.

Period specificity does not require repeating historical exclusion. Human agency, diverse users and public life remain legitimate in a period-reimagined illustration.

### QA gate

Was the modern concept *translated* into a period machine or merely decorated? Is the city functional, the invention understandable, the human active, the grid disciplined, the print material credible and the representation dignified? Are anachronistic smartphones, neon cyberpunk, glossy contemporary CGI, clone-like historical ads or unreadable text present?

**Example:** An imaginary 1974 municipal information network as a civic terminal installed near transit, with printed route cards and a family consulting the system. This expresses public access rather than a present-day map app inside an orange frame.
